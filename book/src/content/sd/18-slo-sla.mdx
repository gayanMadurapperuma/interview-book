"Make it reliable" isn't a goal you can measure. Site Reliability Engineering (SRE) replaces it with three precise terms. An **SLI** (service level indicator) is a measurement — for example, the percentage of requests that succeed within 300 ms. An **SLO** (service level objective) is the target for that measurement — 99.9% over 30 days. An **SLA** (service level agreement) is a contract with customers that says what happens — usually refunds — if a promised level isn't met. The difference between 100% and the SLO is the **error budget**: the amount of unreliability you're allowed, and can spend on deploying changes.

<Analogy>
  A train company. The SLI is "the percentage of trains arriving within 5 minutes of schedule". The SLO is the internal goal: 98% this month. The SLA is the public promise: "if fewer than 95% are on time, season-ticket holders get a discount". The error budget is the 2% of late trains the operator can "afford" — if they're already at 2% by mid-month, they postpone track maintenance until next month.
</Analogy>

## Why it matters

Senior and SRE-flavoured interviews ask how you'd define reliability for a service, what you'd alert on, and how you'd balance shipping speed against stability. Error budgets give a principled answer.

## Choosing good SLIs

Measure what **users** experience, as close to them as possible (at the load balancer or client, not CPU usage).

| Service type | Typical SLIs |
|---|---|
| Request/response API | **availability**: % of requests not returning 5xx · **latency**: % of requests under X ms |
| Data pipeline | **freshness**: % of time data is less than N minutes old · **correctness** |
| Storage | **durability**: % of written objects readable later |
| Queue consumer | % of messages processed within N seconds |

Write SLIs as **good events ÷ valid events**:

```text
availability SLI = successful requests (non-5xx) ÷ all requests
latency SLI      = requests served in < 300 ms ÷ all requests
```

Use percentiles for latency (p95, p99) rather than averages, which hide the slow tail.

## Error budgets

With a 99.9% SLO over 30 days:

- by time: 0.1% × 30 days ≈ **43 minutes** of full outage, or
- by requests: **1 failed request per 1,000**.

The budget becomes a **policy** shared by product and engineering:

<Diagram
  caption="An error-budget policy turns reliability into an objective decision rule about release speed."
  chart={`flowchart TB
    M["Measure SLI vs SLO<br/>over a rolling window"] --> Q{"Error budget<br/>remaining?"}
    Q -- "plenty" --> F["Ship features,<br/>experiment, take risks"]

    Q -- "exhausted" --> S["Freeze risky launches;<br/>prioritise reliability work"]`}
/>

This settles the eternal argument between "ship faster" and "be more careful": if users are happy (budget left), ship; if not, fix reliability first. It also discourages aiming for 100%: perfect reliability is impossibly expensive, and users can't tell 99.99% from 100% when their own Wi-Fi is less reliable than that.

## Alerting on SLOs

Instead of alerting on every CPU spike, alert when the error budget is being **burned** too fast. The **burn rate** is how fast you're consuming the budget relative to plan (burn rate 1 = exactly on budget for the window).

| Alert | Condition (30-day SLO) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Page now | burn rate > 14 over 1 h (and over 5 min) | ~2% of the monthly budget gone in an hour |
| Page | burn rate > 6 over 6 h | sustained serious problem |
| Ticket | burn rate > 1 over 3 days | slow leak; fix during work hours |

Multi-window alerts (long and short windows) catch real problems quickly while avoiding pages for brief blips.

## SLOs in system design

Use SLOs to drive architecture decisions:

- An API with a 99.95% SLO depending on four services in series needs each of them comfortably above that, or the dependency must be made optional (see *Availability*).
- A 300 ms p99 latency SLO dictates timeout budgets for each downstream call.
- A freshness SLO of "5 minutes" for search tells you asynchronous indexing is acceptable.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Common mistakes">

- Measuring server metrics (CPU, memory) instead of user-facing SLIs.
- Setting SLOs to 100% or to whatever the system currently achieves, without asking what users need.
- SLA equal to SLO — no safety margin.
- Using averages for latency.
- Having SLOs nobody acts on (no error-budget policy).

</Callout>

## Explaining it in an interview

<InterviewAnswer question="How would you define and measure reliability for a service?">

I'd pick a few user-facing SLIs, expressed as good events over total events — for an API, the fraction of requests that don't fail and the fraction served under a latency threshold like 300 ms — measured at the load balancer. Then I'd set SLOs for them, such as 99.9% over a rolling 30 days, based on what users actually need rather than what's technically possible. One minus the SLO is the error budget, about 43 minutes a month at 99.9%. We alert on how fast the budget is burning, using multi-window burn-rate alerts instead of raw CPU thresholds, and agree on a policy: while budget remains we ship freely; when it's exhausted we pause risky launches and invest in reliability. Any external SLA is set looser than the SLO to leave margin.

<Fragment slot="deeper">

- **SLI vs SLO vs SLA?** Measurement vs internal target vs contractual promise with penalties.
- **Why not aim for 100%?** Exponential cost, no room for change, and users can't perceive it.
- **What is burn rate?** How fast the error budget is consumed relative to the allowed pace.

</Fragment>
</InterviewAnswer>
