Many problems in distributed systems boil down to **agreement**: which node is the leader? Was this transaction committed? Who holds the lock? **Consensus** algorithms (Raft, Paxos) let a group of nodes agree on a value so that once agreed, it never changes — as long as a **majority** of nodes is working. **Quorums** are the general idea underneath: require enough nodes to participate that any two groups must overlap.

<Analogy>
  A committee of five deciding by majority vote. Any decision needs three votes. Since any two groups of three people share at least one member, two contradictory decisions can't both pass — someone would have to vote for both. And the committee keeps working even if two members are on holiday, but not three.
</Analogy>

## Why it matters

Consensus sits under the tools you depend on: etcd (Kubernetes' brain), ZooKeeper, Consul, Kafka's KRaft controller, CockroachDB and Spanner replication. Interviewers ask why clusters have 3 or 5 nodes, what a quorum is, how leader election avoids two leaders, and how R + W > N works.

## Majority quorums

With **N** nodes, a majority is ⌊N/2⌋ + 1. Any two majorities overlap in at least one node, so information from one majority decision is always visible to the next.

| Nodes | Majority | Failures tolerated |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | 2 | 1 |
| 4 | 3 | 1 (no gain over 3!) |
| 5 | 3 | 2 |
| 7 | 4 | 3 |

That's why consensus clusters use **odd** numbers — usually 3 or 5. More nodes tolerate more failures but make every write wait for more acknowledgements.

## Raft in brief

Raft is designed to be understandable. Nodes are **followers**, **candidates** or a single **leader**. Time is divided into **terms**.

### Leader election

- The leader sends regular **heartbeats**.
- A follower that misses heartbeats for a randomised **election timeout** (e.g. 150–300 ms) increments the term, votes for itself and asks others to vote.
- Each node votes for at most one candidate per term, and only for a candidate whose log is at least as up to date as its own.
- A candidate with a **majority** of votes becomes leader. Randomised timeouts make split votes rare.

Safety comes from the overlap: any future leader must win a majority vote, and at least one voter has every committed entry — and voters refuse candidates with less complete logs. So committed data survives leader changes.

### Split brain is prevented by terms

If an old leader was cut off by a partition, it can't gather a majority, so it can't commit anything. When it reconnects and sees a higher term, it steps down. A minority side of a partition simply becomes **unavailable** for writes — consensus systems are **CP**.

## Quorums in leaderless systems

Dynamo-style databases (Cassandra, DynamoDB internals, Riak) use quorums without a single leader. With **N** replicas per key, a write waits for **W** acknowledgements and a read queries **R** replicas.

<Diagram
  caption="N = 3, W = 2, R = 2. The write reached replicas A and B; the read asks B and C. They overlap at B, so the read sees the newest version."
  chart={`flowchart LR
    W["write v2<br/>(W = 2)"] --> A[("A: v2")]
    W --> B[("B: v2")]
    W -. "slow / missed" .-> C[("C: v1")]
    R["read (R = 2)"] --> B
    R --> C
    B --> RES["newest = v2 ✓<br/>(and repair C)"]
    C --> RES`}
  steps={[
    { nodes: ["W", "A", "B"], text: "The write succeeds once 2 of 3 replicas acknowledge it. C missed it." },
    { nodes: ["R", "B", "C"], text: "A read contacts 2 replicas — here B and C." },
    { nodes: ["B", "C", "RES"], text: "Because R + W = 4 > N = 3, at least one of them (B) has the newest version. The client takes the newest, and **read repair** updates C." },
  ]}
/>

- **R + W > N** → reads overlap with the latest write (strong-ish consistency, with caveats around concurrent writes and failures).
- **R + W ≤ N** → faster, but reads may miss the latest write (eventual).
- Background **anti-entropy** (Merkle trees) and **hinted handoff** repair replicas that missed writes.

## Using consensus in practice

You rarely implement Raft. You use systems built on it:

| Need | Tool |
|---|---|
| Leader election among your service instances | a lease/lock in etcd, ZooKeeper or Consul; or a DB advisory lock |
| Strongly consistent configuration and service discovery | etcd, Consul |
| Distributed lock for correctness | etcd/ZooKeeper locks with **fencing tokens** |
| Strongly consistent replicated database | CockroachDB, Spanner, TiDB, YugabyteDB (Raft/Paxos per range) |

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Common mistakes">

- Even-sized clusters (4 nodes tolerate no more failures than 3).
- Running consensus clusters across high-latency links for every write without accounting for the latency.
- Leader election via "whoever has the earliest timestamp".
- Distributed locks without expiry, or with expiry but without fencing.

</Callout>

## Explaining it in an interview

<InterviewAnswer question="How do distributed systems agree on a leader or a value?">

They use a consensus algorithm like Raft or Paxos built on majority quorums. In Raft, nodes elect a leader for a term: a follower that misses heartbeats becomes a candidate and needs votes from a majority, and nodes only vote for candidates whose logs are up to date. The leader replicates log entries, and an entry is committed once a majority has stored it. Because any two majorities overlap, a committed entry is always known to the next leader, and an isolated old leader can't commit anything — so there's no split brain, at the cost of the minority side being unavailable. That's why clusters have 3 or 5 nodes. Leaderless databases use read and write quorums with R + W > N for overlap. In practice I use etcd, ZooKeeper or a consensus-based database, and fencing tokens for locks.

<Fragment slot="deeper">

- **Why odd cluster sizes?** An even size adds a node without increasing fault tolerance.
- **What does R + W > N give you?** Every read quorum intersects every write quorum.
- **What is a fencing token?** A monotonically increasing lock number that storage uses to reject stale holders.

</Fragment>
</InterviewAnswer>
