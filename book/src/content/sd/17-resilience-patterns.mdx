Every service depends on others: databases, internal APIs, third-party providers. They **will** be slow or unavailable sometimes. **Resilience patterns** keep a dependency's problem from becoming your outage. The core four are **timeouts** (don't wait forever), **retries** (try transient failures again), **circuit breakers** (stop calling something that's clearly broken) and **bulkheads** (don't let one dependency consume all your resources). Around them sit **fallbacks**, **load shedding** and **graceful degradation**.

<Analogy>
  Ordering from a restaurant's delivery partners. You don't wait four hours for one courier (timeout). If an order fails because of a glitch, you try once more — but not fifty times in a second (retry with backoff). If one courier company has failed your last ten orders, you stop using it for a while (circuit breaker). And you never send all your orders through one company, so their bad day doesn't stop your whole kitchen (bulkhead).
</Analogy>

## Why it matters

Cascading failures — one slow dependency taking down everything upstream — are among the most common causes of large outages. Interviewers expect these patterns in any design with service-to-service calls, and like to probe the subtle parts: retry storms, timeout budgets, and how a breaker recovers.

## Timeouts

Every remote call needs one. Choose it from the **latency budget**: if your endpoint must respond within 1 s and calls three things in sequence, each can't take 30 s.

- Base it on the dependency's observed p99 plus headroom, not on a round number.
- Propagate **deadlines**: pass the remaining time downstream (gRPC deadlines, a header), so deeper services don't keep working on requests that have already timed out upstream.
- Make sure the timeout actually **cancels** the work (abort the socket), not just stops waiting.

## Retries

Retries fix transient failures (a dropped connection, a brief failover) — and can turn a small problem into a big one.

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| Retry only **idempotent** operations (or use idempotency keys) | avoid duplicate side effects |
| Retry only **retryable** errors (timeouts, 503, connection resets) — not 400/404 | a bad request won't get better |
| Use **exponential backoff** with **jitter** | spread retries out so clients don't hammer in sync |
| **Cap** attempts and total time | don't exceed the caller's deadline |
| Retry at **one layer** only | 3 retries at each of 3 layers = up to 64× load |
| Use a **retry budget** (e.g. retries ≤ 10% of requests) | prevents retry storms when everything fails |

## Circuit breakers

A circuit breaker wraps calls to a dependency and tracks failures. When failures cross a threshold, it **opens**: further calls fail **immediately** without touching the dependency, giving it room to recover and freeing your resources. After a cool-down it lets a few **trial** calls through (**half-open**); if they succeed, it closes again.

<Diagram
  caption="Circuit breaker states and transitions."
  chart={`stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> Closed
    Closed --> Closed: success / occasional failure
    Closed --> Open: failure rate > threshold (e.g. 50% of last 20 calls)
    Open --> HalfOpen: cool-down elapsed (e.g. 30 s)
    HalfOpen --> Closed: trial calls succeed
    HalfOpen --> Open: a trial call fails`}
/>

When open, return a **fallback**: cached data, a default ("recommendations unavailable"), a queued-for-later response, or a fast error. Breakers should be per dependency (and sometimes per endpoint or host).

## Bulkheads

Named after a ship's watertight compartments: isolate resources so one flood doesn't sink the ship.

- Separate **connection pools / concurrency limits** per dependency: the recommendations client may use at most 20 concurrent calls; when full, fail fast.
- Separate **thread pools or worker queues** for critical vs non-critical work.
- Separate **infrastructure** per tenant tier or region (cells).

## Degradation and load shedding

- **Graceful degradation**: decide in advance which features are optional. A product page without reviews is fine; without a price it isn't.
- **Load shedding**: when overloaded, reject excess requests early (`429`/`503`) rather than serving everything slowly. Prioritise — shed analytics before checkout.
- **Rate limiting** protects you from individual noisy clients.

## Putting it together

A typical outbound call stack, innermost first: **timeout** on each attempt → **retry** with backoff and jitter within the deadline → **circuit breaker** → **bulkhead** concurrency limit → **fallback**. Libraries: resilience4j (Java), Polly (.NET), cockatiel or opossum (Node); service meshes (Envoy/Istio) can apply timeouts, retries and outlier ejection at the network level.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Common mistakes">

- No timeout, or the default (often minutes or infinite).
- Retrying everything, at every layer, without jitter.
- A breaker that never opens (thresholds too high) or never closes (no half-open probing).
- One shared connection pool for all dependencies.
- No decision about which features are optional, so every dependency is effectively critical.

</Callout>

## Explaining it in an interview

<InterviewAnswer question="How do you prevent one failing service from taking down the others?">

Slow dependencies are more dangerous than dead ones, because callers hold threads and connections while they wait, and the failure cascades upstream. So every call gets a timeout derived from the latency budget, with deadlines propagated and the work actually cancelled. Transient failures on idempotent calls get a few retries with exponential backoff and jitter, at one layer only and within a retry budget, so retries don't become a storm. A circuit breaker per dependency fails fast when the error rate is high and probes recovery in a half-open state. Bulkheads — separate concurrency limits or pools per dependency — make sure a slow non-critical service can only exhaust its own share. And I decide which features can degrade, serving fallbacks or cached data, and shed load early when overloaded.

<Fragment slot="deeper">

- **Why jitter?** Desynchronises retries so they don't arrive in waves.
- **What is a retry budget?** A cap on retries as a fraction of traffic, preventing amplification.
- **Half-open state?** The breaker lets limited trial traffic through to test recovery.

</Fragment>
</InterviewAnswer>
