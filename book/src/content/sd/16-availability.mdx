**Availability** is the fraction of time a system works correctly. It's usually quoted in "nines": 99.9% (three nines) allows about 8.8 hours of downtime a year; 99.99% about 53 minutes. A system is built from parts, and simple arithmetic shows how they combine: components **in series** (all needed) multiply their availabilities — making the whole **less** available than any part — while **redundant** components in parallel make the whole **more** available. Designing for availability is mostly about removing single points of failure and limiting the blast radius of each failure.

<Analogy>
  Christmas lights. Old-style lights wired in series go dark if any single bulb fails — the more bulbs, the more likely the string is dark. Lights wired in parallel stay lit even if a few bulbs die. Your architecture is a string of lights: every required dependency is a bulb in series.
</Analogy>

## Why it matters

Interviewers ask you to reason about availability targets, single points of failure, multi-AZ and multi-region designs. Being able to say "three dependencies at 99.9% in series give about 99.7%" shows you can quantify trade-offs.

## The nines

| Availability | Downtime per year | per month | per week |
|---|---|---|---|
| 99% | 3.65 days | 7.3 hours | 1.7 hours |
| 99.9% | 8.8 hours | 43.8 minutes | 10 minutes |
| 99.95% | 4.4 hours | 21.9 minutes | 5 minutes |
| 99.99% | 52.6 minutes | 4.4 minutes | 1 minute |
| 99.999% | 5.3 minutes | 26 seconds | 6 seconds |

Each extra nine costs roughly 10× more effort: redundancy everywhere, automated failover, careful deploys, 24/7 on-call. Most products don't need five nines.

- **Series**: A = A₁ × A₂ × … × Aₙ
- **Parallel** (independent): A = 1 − (1 − A₁) × (1 − A₂) × …

The catch is **independence**. Three instances in the same rack, zone or deploy share failures (power, network, a bad release). Redundancy only helps if replicas fail independently — hence multiple availability zones, staggered deploys and diverse dependencies.

Checklist: multiple instances per tier, spread across availability zones; managed load balancer; database with standby and automatic failover; replicated cache; no one-off "special" server; queues and storage on managed, replicated services.

## Failure models

How things fail determines how you defend:

| Failure | Example | Defence |
|---|---|---|
| **Crash (fail-stop)** | process or machine dies | redundancy, health checks, restarts |
| **Omission** | messages lost | retries, acks, idempotency |
| **Timing / gray failure** | node is slow, not dead; passes health checks | timeouts, outlier detection, circuit breakers |
| **Byzantine** | node sends wrong or malicious data | checksums, validation; full BFT only for blockchains/hostile settings |
| **Correlated** | AZ outage, bad config pushed everywhere, expired certificate | multi-AZ/region, staged rollouts, fault isolation |

**Gray failures** — partial, slow, intermittent — cause many real outages because simple health checks still pass. Health checks should test real functionality, and clients should detect slow peers.

## Limiting the blast radius

- **Cells / shards**: split users across independent copies of the stack, so one failure affects a fraction of customers.
- **Bulkheads**: separate pools for different dependencies or customer tiers.
- **Staged rollouts**: canary → one zone → everything; automatic rollback on errors.
- **Graceful degradation**: if recommendations are down, show the page without them.
- **Static stability**: systems keep working with their last known configuration if the control plane fails.

## Multi-region

| Mode | RTO / RPO | Cost / complexity |
|---|---|---|
| Backup & restore in another region | hours / hours | low |
| Pilot light (data replicated, minimal infra) | tens of minutes / minutes | medium |
| Warm standby (scaled-down copy running) | minutes / seconds–minutes | medium–high |
| Active–active (all regions serve traffic) | seconds / near zero | high (write conflicts, routing) |

**RTO** (recovery time objective) is how long recovery may take; **RPO** (recovery point objective) is how much recent data you can afford to lose.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Common mistakes">

- Counting redundant components as independent when they share a zone, deploy or config.
- Adding required dependencies without accounting for the availability cost.
- Health checks that only test "process is up".
- Untested failover and backups — they fail when you need them.
- Aiming for five nines without the budget or the need.

</Callout>

## Explaining it in an interview

<InterviewAnswer question="How do you design a system for high availability?">

I start from a target, like 99.95%, which allows about 22 minutes of downtime a month, and work out the maths: required dependencies in series multiply, so four at 99.9% give only about 99.6%; independent redundant components in parallel multiply failure probabilities, so redundancy raises availability. So I remove single points of failure — several stateless instances across availability zones behind a managed load balancer, a database with a synchronous standby and automatic failover, replicated caches and queues — and reduce the number of hard dependencies, degrading gracefully when optional ones fail. Because failures are often correlated or gray, I use real health checks, timeouts and circuit breakers, staged rollouts, and cell-based isolation to limit the blast radius. Multi-region active-passive or active-active comes in when RTO and RPO demand it.

<Fragment slot="deeper">

- **What is RTO vs RPO?** Time to recover vs amount of data loss allowed.
- **What is a gray failure?** Partial or degraded failure that basic health checks miss.
- **Why must redundancy be independent?** Correlated failures take out all replicas at once.

</Fragment>
</InterviewAnswer>
